From my thesis reading list

Wide & Deep Learning for Recommender Systems
Cheng et al., Google · 2016
Ref. [13] in my paper
The core idea

Recommenders need two abilities that fight each other: memorisation (this user always clicks this exact item) and generalisation (users who like X tend to like Y). Wide & Deep trains a linear model over cross-product features (wide, for memorisation) jointly with a deep neural network over embeddings (deep, for generalisation), combining both in one output.

Why it matters

It shaped how industry thinks about hybrid architectures — and it's a direct ancestor of the dual-network approach I used in my own thesis. The insight that different model components can own different behaviours, rather than forcing one network to do everything, keeps reappearing across modern recsys papers.

One open question

The wide part needs hand-engineered feature crosses. A decade later, how much of that manual work do attention mechanisms actually replace — and at what serving cost?

DeepFM: A Factorization-Machine Based Neural Network for CTR Prediction
Guo et al. · 2017
Ref. [17] in my paper
The core idea

Take Wide & Deep, but remove the manual feature engineering: replace the wide component with a factorization machine that learns pairwise feature interactions automatically, and let the FM and deep components share the same input embeddings.

Why it matters

Shared embeddings mean one representation serves both low-order and high-order interactions — cleaner to train, no feature crosses to design. It's a good example of a pattern I care about: architectural changes that remove human bottlenecks rather than just adding capacity.

One open question

FM captures pairwise interactions elegantly, but real preference structure is often higher-order and conditional. Where exactly does the deep component stop compensating?
